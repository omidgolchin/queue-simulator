How many staff does a queue need? The queueing formula (Erlang C) gives the staffing answer in one line, when its assumptions hold. This page lets you staff a call centre with the formula, then run the simulation and watch where the two agree and where they part company.

Can an LLM do this instead?

Fifteen queue scenarios were put to several open-weight models three ways: estimate directly, write code and run it, and simulate 20 customers by hand, event by event. Ground truth: Erlang C, or 200 replications of the Python simulator.

Why one long run is not enough

Every simulated number here comes from independent replications with a warm-up period and a confidence interval — not from a single long run. Queues are autocorrelated: one busy hour colours the next, so a single run's average hides its own error. The Python simulator was checked against Erlang C on 12 settings (inside the 95% CI in every one) and against Little's law on every run.

The 30-second version

Callers arrive at random, calls take a random time, and a few agents answer. Erlang C turns three numbers (arrival rate, call length, agents) into the average wait and the share of callers waiting over 20 seconds — exactly, if arrivals are Poisson, calls are exponential, nobody hangs up, and the load is steady. Change any of those and you need a simulation.

The intuition

Waits explode as utilisation approaches 100%: going from 80% to 95% busy multiplies the average wait several-fold, because there is no slack left to absorb a burst. That is why the staffing curve bends so steeply, and why averaging a day's arrivals before applying the formula understates the rush-hour waits: the bad hours dominate.

The maths

With offered load a = λ/μ and c agents, Erlang C gives the probability an arrival waits, C(c, a); the mean queue wait is C(c, a) / (cμ − λ), and P(wait > t) = C(c, a)·e−(cμ−λ)t. Little's law ties the averages together for any queue: L = λW. The simulator checks it from its own counters on every replication.
